BACKGROUND

About dyslexia and this experiment

What is dyslexia?

Dyslexia is a learning difference that can mainly affect fluent and accurate reading and spelling. Reading may take more time and effort. It says nothing about a person's intelligence. Experiences and helpful support differ from person to person.

What does this page do?

The inspiration describes one person's experience of reading that takes concentration, and experiments with occasionally swapped letters. This page makes those letter effects adjustable and adds gentle, random movement. It is an artificial example to start a conversation about reading effort, not a depiction of how anyone's brain or vision works.

Use it thoughtfully

Use this as a short demonstration or conversation starter, not as a test, diagnosis, or proof of what dyslexia feels like. Ask people with dyslexia about their own experiences, and let them skip or pause the effects. Font, spacing, and colour settings are personal preferences; they are not treatment and do not work the same way for everyone.

RESEARCH AND EXPERIENCE

A closer look at dyslexia

This short overview draws on research and health information. It is general information, not a diagnosis or a description that fits everyone.

Why can reading take more effort?

Reading involves recognising written words, linking letters with speech sounds, and building meaning from sentences. For some people with dyslexia, recognising words accurately or quickly is less automatic. More attention may go into working out words, leaving less available to hold on to the meaning of a long sentence. A reader may go back and read it again, even when they understand the ideas perfectly well.

For example, one reader may recognise a long word straight away, while another needs to sound it out and then reread the sentence to reconnect with its meaning. This is only an illustration: people with dyslexia read in many different ways.

Possible experiences in daily life

Reading and spelling may be slow or tiring; written instructions, unfamiliar names, or reading aloud may take extra effort. After a long text, someone may feel mentally tired, lose the thread, or need a break. Other people may mainly notice spelling or planning tasks, and some people experience few difficulties in particular situations.

Letters do not generally move or reverse for everyone with dyslexia. Some people do report visual discomfort or difficulty following lines, but that is not a universal feature or proof of dyslexia. The moving-letter effects on this site are an optional illustration, not a simulation.

What research says, with context

Dyslexia mainly affects word reading and/or spelling, including accuracy and reading speed. It occurs across a range of abilities and is not a measure of intelligence. A research review estimated that about 7 in 100 primary-school-age children met the study criteria worldwide; estimates depend on language, methods, and definitions, so this is not an exact figure for the Netherlands or for diagnosed people.

Reading difficulties can be associated with stress, reading fatigue, anxiety, or lower confidence for some people. An association does not mean dyslexia inevitably causes mental-health problems. Supportive teaching, understanding, and suitable tools can make a difference.

Other needs and useful support

Dyslexia can occur alongside ADHD, developmental language disorder, or dyscalculia, but these are distinct conditions and not everyone has more than one. Difficulties with memory, organisation, or processing speed may also occur for some people; they are not requirements for dyslexia.

Helpful support depends on the person. It can include structured reading and spelling instruction, extra time, clear step-by-step directions, text-to-speech, audiobooks, or adjustments at school, college, or work. A qualified specialist can assess reading and spelling needs; an online demonstration cannot diagnose dyslexia.
